// UNDERSTAND THE OBSTACLE

Why a familiar part becomes difficult to find.

A standard part number does not guarantee that material will be available when you need it.

Production may have stopped, a manufacturing source may have disappeared, or raw materials may have become unavailable. These are among the supply problems addressed by the Defense Logistics Agency’s obsolescence and material shortage program.1

The obstacle may also be specific to your purchase: the required quantity, delivery date, approved manufacturer, or documentation.

Before widening the search, identify what your current supplier cannot support. “No stock,” “delivery too late,” and “manufacturer records unavailable” call for different follow-up questions.

// A BETTER STARTING POINT

Send the requirement behind the number.

Give the distributor enough information to evaluate the actual purchase. Include:

  • Complete part numberPreserve every prefix, dash number, and suffix.
  • Quantity and timingState the total requirement and the first quantity you need.
  • Drawing or specificationInclude the applicable revision and relevant notes.
  • Source restrictionsIdentify required manufacturers or customer-approved sources.
  • DocumentationSpecify manufacturer certificates, traceability records, or reports.
  • Previous purchasing informationInclude a known manufacturer number, packaging label, or earlier purchase order if identification is uncertain.

Avoid reducing a specified item to a description such as “stainless screw.” Keep the original designation attached to the inquiry.

// PLAN THE DELIVERY

Separate the immediate shortage from the full requirement.

Tell the distributor whether you need the entire quantity at once or could accept an initial shipment followed by scheduled deliveries.

Ask for available options to be quoted separately. That makes it easier to compare a complete delivery with a proposed split shipment.

For recurring demand, include estimated usage and future release dates. Ask whether planned purchases, stocking arrangements, or scheduled releases could suit the requirement. The objective is to address the next purchase as well as the immediate shortage.

// KEEP APPROVALS CLEAR

Review alternatives with the right people.

An unfamiliar designation does not automatically mean a different part.

The Aerospace Industries Association explains that military standards converted to NASM or NAM documents can retain MS procurement part numbers. Check the applicable document rather than changing prefixes yourself.2

Keep any proposed alternative separate from the exact requirement. Ask what differs and send that information to your engineer or responsible approval authority before accepting a change.

Availability is one part of the decision. Your drawing, source restrictions, and acceptance requirements still control the purchase.
